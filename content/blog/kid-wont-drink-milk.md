# My Kid Won't Drink Milk. Here's What the Growth Science Says to Do.

At some point, a lot of kids announce that milk is over. Sometimes it's an allergy. Sometimes it's lactose. Sometimes it's a six-year-old who tried it warm once and has filed it under "never again."

Whatever the reason, the same thing happens next, and it happens quietly. Let's look at what actually leaves the diet when milk does, and then fix it.

## Try this: the milk subtraction

Get a piece of paper. Write down what your kid ate and drank yesterday. Now cross out every glass of milk.

Look at what's left and ask two questions. Where's the protein now? Where's the calcium? For a lot of kids, the honest answer is: in the crossed-out part.

That's the problem with milk. It was doing three jobs in one glass, and when it goes, all three go at once.

## What milk was actually delivering

One cup of milk, roughly:

- **8 grams of protein.** For a first grader who needs about 19 a day, that's nearly half.
- **300 mg of calcium.** Kids 4 to 8 need about 1,000 mg; ages 9 to 18 need 1,300. One cup is a quarter to a third of the day.
- **Vitamin D**, if it's fortified. The nutrient that lets calcium into bone at all.

And there's something else, which is why milk in particular shows up in growth research.

## Why the growth research keeps pointing at milk

In 2004, researchers in Denmark measured 90 healthy toddlers: what they ate for a week, how tall they were, and how much IGF-1 was in their blood. IGF-1 is the messenger hormone that tells growth plates to build bone.

The kids who drank more milk were taller and had more IGF-1. Going from about one cup a day to two and a half went with 30% more of it. And here is the detail that matters for you: **meat protein and vegetable protein didn't show the same link. Milk did.**

Then in 2012, a meta-analysis pooled twelve controlled trials where children were actually given extra dairy. Result: about 0.4 cm of extra growth per year for each cup of milk a day. Strongest in teenagers and in kids running short for their age.

So when milk leaves the diet, it isn't only calories leaving. It's a specific, well-documented set of growth nutrients. Which is the good news, because a specific set is a set you can replace.

## Replacing what left, in order of importance

### 1. Protein, spread across the day

The body doesn't store protein for later. It uses what shows up at each meal. So the goal isn't a big dinner; it's some protein at breakfast, lunch, and after school.

Eggs (6 g), yogurt if dairy's still okay (8 to 12 g), nut butter (4 g per tablespoon), beans, chicken, a cheese stick (7 g). And a drink that carries protein, for the moments food isn't happening.

### 2. Calcium, most days

If dairy is still on the table in other forms, yogurt and cheese carry most of what milk did. If dairy's out entirely: fortified plant milks (check the label; many have 300 mg per cup, some have far less), fortified orange juice, tofu set with calcium, canned salmon with bones, and leafy greens for the rare kid who eats them.

### 3. Vitamin D, so the calcium works

Fortified foods, fatty fish, eggs, sunlight in the warm months. In winter at northern latitudes, ask your pediatrician about a supplement. A lot of kids are low and nobody checks.

Don't. A daily standoff over a glass of milk teaches a kid that milk is a punishment, and it rarely ends with more milk consumed. Replace the nutrients through routes they'll take. If they come back to milk at nine, great. If they don't, they still grew.

## FAQ

**What can I give my child instead of milk?**
Cover the three things milk provided: protein (eggs, yogurt, cheese, beans, meat, or a protein-bearing drink), calcium (fortified plant milks or juices, yogurt, cheese, tofu, canned fish), and vitamin D (fortified foods, fish, sunlight, or a supplement your pediatrician recommends).

**How much calcium does a child need without milk?**
About 1,000 mg a day for ages 4 to 8 and 1,300 mg for ages 9 to 18. One cup of milk is about 300 mg, so replacing it means roughly a quarter to a third of the day's calcium from other sources.

**Will my child grow normally without milk?**
Yes, if the nutrients milk provides come from elsewhere. The growth research points at protein, calcium, and vitamin D, not at milk as such. Track growth at checkups and raise it with your pediatrician if the curve flattens.

**Is plant milk as good as cow's milk for kids?**
It depends on the carton. Fortified soy milk is closest on protein (7 to 8 g); most almond and oat milks have 1 to 3 g. Check calcium and vitamin D fortification on the label; it varies a lot.
